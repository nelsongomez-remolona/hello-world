## 1. Trump, Xi and Putin meet in Shenzhen

**What happened.** On the sidelines of APEC in Shenzhen, Xi [greeted Trump on the tarmac](https://www.justsecurity.org/165038/early-edition-november-18-2026/), which breaks with protocol, and held separate bilaterals with [Trump and with Putin](https://aa.com.tr/en/world/morning-briefing-nov-18-2026/4128568). All three then met at Kylin Villa. Chinese officials [call it a "trilateral meeting," not a summit](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-19/trump-xi-putin-meeting-live-blog-apec-summit/107394202). [According to Chinese state media](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/liveblog/2026/11/19/apec-live-trump-xi-and-putin-to-meet-in-historic-three-way-china-summit), the leaders committed to "stable energy flows" in a "peace declaration," and reports mention nuclear talks. No text, scope or timeline has been published. Separately, China [extended its suspension of a 24% tariff on US goods to 10 January 2027](https://gcaptain.com/china-extends-us-trade-truce-by-two-months/), keeping a 10% levy in place. The [first US–China Joint Trade Council meeting](https://moderndiplomacy.eu/2026/11/15/can-china-and-the-us-settle-the-tariff-fight-at-their-first-trade-council-meeting/) on 13 November ended without a deal.

**Background.** APEC is a forum of 21 Pacific Rim economies. Its decisions aren't binding, and its real value is that it puts leaders in the same place, which is why the sideline meetings matter more than the official agenda. On nuclear weapons, arms control has been two-sided for fifty years: US–Soviet, then US–Russian treaties capped each side's arsenal. China has always refused to join, arguing that its arsenal is a fraction of the US and Russian ones. Any three-way nuclear process would end that refusal.

**Why it matters.** There are two readings. *Optimistic:* a standing three-way channel lowers the risk of miscalculation among the three largest nuclear powers, and an energy deal gives Russia and China supply stability while Hormuz is unreliable. *Skeptical (held by many US allies):* making China the host and broker between Washington and Moscow raises Beijing's leverage, and a "peace declaration" without published terms commits nobody to anything. Which reading holds depends on whether anything specific follows.

**Confidence.** The meeting is confirmed by multiple outlets. The declaration's content comes only from Chinese state media. No US or Russian readout was available.

**Watch.** A US or Russian readout, or a published text. Whether the nuclear item becomes a named process with dates. The [G20 summit at Doral, Florida, on 14–15 Dec](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/trump-g20-summit-2026-doral-resort-florida), and whether Xi and Putin attend.

## 2. Gulf: renewed US–Iran strikes; the IEA says Hormuz has been permanently re-priced

**What happened.** On 17 Nov the US [struck an Iranian missile-launch site on Qeshm Island](https://gulfnews.com/world/mena/middle-east-war-iran-attacks-bahrain-kuwait-drone-threats-keep-gulf-on-high-alert-as-us-hits-irans-qeshm-1.500751444) in the Strait of Hormuz, calling it self-defense after repeated Iranian attacks on Bahrain. Early on 18 Nov, Iran [said it launched drones at Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait](https://aa.com.tr/en/world/morning-briefing-nov-18-2026/4128568), which hosts US forces. Kuwait said it was engaging "hostile" drones but did not confirm any hits. Yemen's Houthis [said they sent five drones](https://www.justsecurity.org/165038/early-edition-november-18-2026/) toward pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East–West pipeline. IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol [said there is no "credible pathway"](https://cnbc.com/2026/11/18/iea-strait-hormuz-energy-risk.html) back to pre-war normality: a closure has now happened once, so risk perceptions have "changed for good." In the same report, the IEA put Gulf crude exports in October at more than 80% of pre-war levels. Brent [traded around $92](https://finance.yahoo.com/energy/articles/oil-prices-decline-rising-us-102355771.html) on 18 Nov. It fell despite the strikes because US inventories rose.

**Background.** Hormuz is a chokepoint. Before the war it carried roughly a fifth of the world's oil consumption, plus most of Qatar's LNG exports, and there are few alternative routes. The main bypasses are Saudi Arabia's East–West pipeline to the Red Sea and the UAE's pipeline to Fujairah, which together carry only part of normal volumes. The IEA was created after the 1973 oil embargo. It requires member countries to hold emergency oil stocks equal to 90 days of net imports and coordinates releasing them, which is what [cushioned this year's shock](https://cnbc.com/2026/11/18/iea-strait-hormuz-energy-risk.html).

**Why it matters.** Birol's point is that **flow numbers and risk numbers have come apart**. Oil is moving again, but insurers, importers and lenders now price in the chance of a second closure. That shows up as higher insurance, larger stockpiles (the Philippines and Thailand are [expanding fuel buffers](https://cnbc.com/2026/11/18/iea-strait-hormuz-energy-risk.html)), more bypass pipelines, and more policy support for domestic power sources. The Houthi drones targeted a bypass pipeline, which shows the weakness of that strategy: the alternative route is also a target. See today's Concept below.

**Confidence.** The strikes are confirmed by multiple outlets. Iran's claimed target is Iran's own statement. Damage assessments come from interested parties. The IEA figures are primary data.

**Watch.** Whether Iranian strikes spread beyond Kuwait and Bahrain. Tanker insurance rates. The IEA's December oil market report.

## 3. "Java4Shell": an AI-found flaw and the patching rush behind the US outages

**What happened.** The security firm Calif, using AI-assisted analysis, found a [memory-corruption bug in OpenJDK's Base64 decoder](https://blog.qualys.com/vulnerabilities-threat-research/2026/11/17/how-to-find-mitigate-and-patch-the-critical-java4shell-flaw-cve-2026-114938-now) (CVE-2026-114938). It allows remote code execution against common products in their default settings. CISA [issued an alert on 17 Nov](https://blog.qualys.com/vulnerabilities-threat-research/2026/11/17/how-to-find-mitigate-and-patch-the-critical-java4shell-flaw-cve-2026-114938-now) calling for immediate patching. After disruptions at Microsoft, the FAA, banks and NSA communications, Secretary of State Rubio [said there was "zero evidence"](https://www.justsecurity.org/165038/early-edition-november-18-2026/) of a cyberattack. He and DNI Jay Clayton pointed instead to the rush to patch systems before AI-enabled attackers could exploit the flaw. The FAA [paused installation of a router type](https://www.justsecurity.org/165038/early-edition-november-18-2026/) linked to last Friday's nationwide ground stop.

**Background.** A *CVE* is the public ID number given to a known software flaw. *Remote code execution* is the most serious class of flaw: an attacker can run their own commands on your machine over the network. The nickname echoes **Log4Shell** (December 2021), a flaw in a widely used Java logging library that took organizations months to find and fix, because the vulnerable code was buried inside thousands of other products. Java runs much of the world's banking, airline, government and enterprise back-end software. Patching it is slow because, [as Qualys notes](https://blog.qualys.com/vulnerabilities-threat-research/2026/11/17/how-to-find-mitigate-and-patch-the-critical-java4shell-flaw-cve-2026-114938-now), a fix only takes effect when each running Java program is restarted.

**Why it matters.** AI tools have made finding serious flaws far cheaper, but fixing them still requires slow, risky restarts of production systems, and rushed fixes cause outages of their own. For anyone running infrastructure, how quickly systems can be safely patched and restarted is becoming a core security measure. The official explanation (patching, not attack) is plausible but hasn't been independently verified.

**Confidence.** The CVE and CISA alert are primary sources. The claim that the outages came from patching rather than an attack is the US government's assessment.

**Watch.** The release of the OpenJDK fix and Oracle's November Critical Patch Update (both "TBD" as of 17 Nov). Any confirmed exploitation. The [AI-security hearings](https://www.justsecurity.org/165038/early-edition-november-18-2026/) being prepared by incoming Senate Homeland Security chair Maggie Hassan.

## 4. Several central banks are still raising rates into an energy shock

**What happened.** In November the Reserve Bank of Australia [raised its rate 25bp to 4.85%](https://www.babypips.com/news/headline-rba-interest-rate-decision-november-2026-fifth-hike-split-vote) on a 6–3 vote, its fifth hike of this cycle. Secondary aggregators report 25bp hikes by the [Bank of England and Czech National Bank](https://tradingeconomics.com/news/interest-rate) to 4%, an unexpected Mexican cut to 6.25%, and a Brazilian pause at 13.75%. The Fed's [minutes released in October](https://www.ksat.com/business/2026/10/07/fed-minutes-another-rate-hike-likely-coming-this-year-to-combat-persistent-inflation/) showed most officials expecting another hike in 2026, with inflation having made little progress toward 2%. Spot gold [fell to about $3,792/oz](https://aa.com.tr/en/world/morning-briefing-nov-18-2026/4128568), more than 25% below its level when the war began in late February.

**Background.** Central banks distinguish two kinds of inflation. *Demand-driven* inflation (too much spending) responds well to higher rates. A *supply shock* such as an oil disruption raises prices while also slowing the economy, and the textbook advice is to "look through" it. The exception is when it feeds into *second-round effects*: workers win higher wages and companies raise prices because they expect inflation to continue. The *real interest rate* is the policy rate minus inflation. It is what actually tightens or loosens credit, and it is the main cost of holding gold, which pays no interest.

**Why it matters.** There are two readings. *Hawkish (the majority view at the RBA and BoE):* after inflation that started in the early 2020s and was then hit by tariffs and the war, expectations are fragile, so second-round effects are the bigger risk, and stopping now would cost more later. *Dovish (the three RBA dissenters, and Mexico's decision):* rate rises do nothing about oil supply and add a recession on top of an energy shock. Higher real rates also explain gold's unusual fall during a war, since holding gold now means giving up a positive return. Higher rates in several large economies at once raise borrowing costs for indebted governments and for emerging markets that borrow in dollars.

**Confidence.** The RBA decision is confirmed. The BoE, CNB, Mexico and Brazil figures come from aggregators and need checking against each bank's own statement. Gold is market data.

**Watch.** The contents of the Fed minutes released 18 Nov. Next US CPI release. The ECB's December meeting. Fed Governor Lisa Cook's [dismissal hearing on 14 Dec](https://www.justsecurity.org/165038/early-edition-november-18-2026/), a test of Fed independence.

## 5. Trade after the IEEPA ruling: allies hedge

**What happened.** On the APEC sidelines, Canada's Mark Carney and Xi [agreed to expand trade through reciprocal tariff relief](https://aa.com.tr/en/world/morning-briefing-nov-18-2026/4128568) and new commercial deals, despite US tariff threats. US–Canada talks over [steel (up to 50%) and auto (25%) tariffs](https://thehub.ca/2026/11/13/u-s-trade-chief-confirms-recent-talks-with-canada-but-sets-no-deadline-for-a-deal/) have no deadline. Carney [says he will meet Trump](https://www.justsecurity.org/165038/early-edition-november-18-2026/) and that a deal is possible. In Manila, Trump [announced a bilateral trade agreement and expanded defense cooperation](https://aa.com.tr/en/world/morning-briefing-nov-18-2026/4128568) with the Philippines, and the [Quad held its first leaders' meeting in over two years](https://aa.com.tr/en/world/morning-briefing-nov-18-2026/4128568).

**Background.** US presidents can impose tariffs under several laws. In 2025 the administration relied mostly on **IEEPA**, a 1977 emergency-powers statute never before used for tariffs. On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court [ruled 6–3 in *Learning Resources v. Trump*](https://ballotpedia.org/Learning_Resources,_Inc._v._Trump) that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs, and the [IEEPA tariffs were ended that day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_Resources,_Inc._v._Trump). The ruling [did not touch tariffs under other statutes](https://www.blakes.com/insights/u-s-supreme-court-strikes-down-certain-tariffs-raising-new-uncertainty-for-canada-u-s-trade/), most importantly **Section 232** (national-security tariffs, which cover the steel and auto duties on Canada) and **Section 301** (unfair trade practices, which covers much of the China tariff structure). These statutes are narrower: each requires an investigation of a specific product or country. As a result, tariff policy is now more targeted and slower to change, and negotiations focus on specific sectors.

**Why it matters.** US allies are deepening security ties with Washington (the Philippines, the Quad) while reducing their trade exposure to it, including with China. Canada, a G7 member, cutting reciprocal tariffs with China is the clearest example. For companies, the IEEPA ruling replaced broad, overnight tariff risk with narrower risk by sector, which makes it easier to tell which supply chains are exposed.

**Confidence.** The Canada–China and US–Philippines announcements are confirmed. Their detailed terms have not been published.

**Watch.** The 10 January 2027 end of China's tariff suspension. Any new Section 232 investigations. Whether the Carney–Trump meeting produces a framework.

## 6. Ukraine: talks in Berlin, fighting expected to continue through winter

**What happened.** US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, Ukrainian officials and European allies are [meeting in Berlin this week](https://kyivindependent.com/zelensky-announces-berlin-talks-with-us-european-allies-to-shape-next-phase-of-russia-negotiations/). German Chancellor Merz is hosting a Coalition of the Willing summit. [Per the New York Times](https://www.justsecurity.org/165038/early-edition-november-18-2026/), Western intelligence assesses that Putin intends to keep fighting at least through winter. Germany [announced new aid](https://aa.com.tr/en/world/morning-briefing-nov-18-2026/4128568): air defense, guided missiles and F-16 ammunition. Germany's defense minister Boris Pistorius [publicly rejected](https://www.justsecurity.org/165038/early-edition-november-18-2026/) US Under Secretary Elbridge Colby's criticism of Ukrainian strikes on Russian logistics depots.

**Background.** The *Coalition of the Willing* is a European-led group of countries, formed in 2025, that is planning security guarantees and a possible reassurance force for Ukraine after any ceasefire. Its existence reflects European doubt that US guarantees alone would be enough. Winter matters because Russia has repeatedly targeted Ukraine's power, heating and water infrastructure in the cold months, and Ukraine's ability to keep the grid running depends on air-defense interceptors, which are in short supply.

**Why it matters.** The Berlin meeting sets the position of Ukraine and its allies. Talks with Russia only produce results if Moscow wants a settlement, and the intelligence assessment says it doesn't, at least not before spring. In practice, the winter question is how many interceptors Ukraine has. The open US–German disagreement over which Ukrainian strikes inside Russia are acceptable shows the allies have not agreed on how much military pressure on Russia is appropriate.

**Confidence.** The Berlin meetings are confirmed. The intelligence assessment comes from unnamed sources reported by a single outlet.

**Watch.** A communiqué from the Berlin summit. Finnish President Stubb's [call for a fixed timetable](https://aa.com.tr/en/world/morning-briefing-nov-18-2026/4128568) and agenda for negotiations. Russian strikes on energy infrastructure as temperatures fall.

## Concept: the risk premium that outlasts the risk

When a shipping route, supplier or country fails once, markets don't go back to the old price once things work again. They add a **risk premium**: extra cost charged for the possibility that the failure happens again. It appears in insurance rates, inventory levels (companies hold more stock "just in case" rather than "just in time"), the yields lenders demand, and investment decisions (building a pipeline around the chokepoint even when the chokepoint is open).

This is what [Birol is describing](https://cnbc.com/2026/11/18/iea-strait-hormuz-energy-risk.html) for Hormuz. Gulf exports can recover to 80% or 100% of pre-war levels while the system costs more to run, because every refiner, utility and finance ministry now plans for a second closure. The same reasoning explains why companies keep moving supply chains away from China during tariff pauses, and why a central bank that has lost credibility on inflation has to keep rates higher than the current data alone would justify.

**Question to ask of any "things are back to normal" headline:** has the price of insuring against a repeat come back down? If not, the event is still costing money.
