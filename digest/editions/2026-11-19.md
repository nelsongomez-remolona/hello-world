## 1. Trump, Xi and Putin meet in Shenzhen

**What happened.** On the APEC sidelines in Shenzhen, Xi held a bilateral with Trump (after greeting him on the tarmac himself, which breaks with protocol) and one with Putin. All three then met at Kylin Villa. Chinese officials call it a "trilateral meeting," not a summit. According to Chinese state media, the leaders committed to "stable energy flows" among the three countries in what was described as a "peace declaration." Reports also mention nuclear talks. No text, scope, or timeline has been published. Separately, the US–China tariff truce was extended by two months in early November. The first US–China Joint Trade Council meeting (13 Nov) ended without a deal.

**Why it matters.** Until now, great-power diplomacy has run as a set of separate bilateral channels. A standing three-way format would let China act as host and broker between Washington and Moscow, which raises its leverage over both. An energy-flow commitment matters most for Russia, which needs buyers for its oil and gas, and for China, which wants supply security while Hormuz is unreliable. Arms-control talks would be the first serious nuclear negotiation involving China. China has always refused trilateral arms-control talks, so even agreeing to talk would be a change.

**Confidence.** The meeting itself is confirmed by multiple outlets. The declaration's content comes only from Chinese state media. No US or Russian readout was available at time of writing.

**Watch.** A US or Russian readout, and whether a text is published. Whether the nuclear item becomes a named process with dates. The G20 leaders' summit at Doral, Florida, on 14–15 Dec, and whether Putin and Xi attend.

## 2. Gulf: renewed US–Iran strikes; IEA says Hormuz has been permanently re-priced

**What happened.** On Tuesday (17 Nov), the US struck an Iranian missile-launch site on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, calling it self-defense after repeated Iranian attacks on Bahrain. Early Wednesday, Iran said it launched drones at Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait, which hosts US forces. Kuwait said it was engaging "hostile" drones but did not confirm any hits. Bahrain intercepted several attacks. On 17 Nov, Yemen's Houthis said they sent five drones toward pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East–West pipeline. US–Iran talks are stalled over safe navigation through Hormuz. The foreign ministers of Oman, Iran and Uzbekistan issued a joint call for freedom of navigation.

IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol said there is no "credible pathway" back to pre-war normality in the strait. His argument: a closure has now happened once, so risk perceptions have "changed for good." The IEA's November report says Gulf crude exports recovered to more than 80% of pre-war levels in October. Brent traded around $92 on Wednesday. It fell rather than rose despite the strikes, because US inventories increased.

**Why it matters.** Roughly a fifth of global oil and a large share of LNG normally pass through Hormuz. Birol's point is that **the flow numbers and the risk numbers have come apart**: oil can move again while insurers, importers and lenders still price in the chance of another closure. That shows up as higher shipping insurance, bigger strategic stockpiles (the Philippines and Thailand, the hardest-hit importers, are already expanding fuel buffers), more investment in Saudi and Emirati bypass pipelines, and faster policy support for domestic power sources, including nuclear. Note that the Houthi drones targeted the East–West pipeline, which is the main bypass route. One drawback of bypass routes is that they are also targets.

**Confidence.** The strikes are confirmed by multiple outlets. Iran's claimed target is Iran's own statement. Damage assessments come from interested parties on both sides. The IEA figures are primary data.

**Watch.** Whether Iranian strikes on Gulf states spread beyond Kuwait and Bahrain. Tanker insurance rates. The IEA's December oil market report.

## 3. "Java4Shell": an AI-found flaw and the patching rush behind the US outages

**What happened.** The security firm Calif, using AI-assisted analysis, found a memory-corruption bug in OpenJDK's Base64 decoder (CVE-2026-114938, nicknamed "Java4Shell"). It allows remote code execution against common products in their default configurations. CISA issued an emergency patching alert on 17 Nov. Recent disruptions at Microsoft, the FAA, major banks and NSA communications prompted speculation about an attack. Secretary of State Rubio said there was "zero evidence" of one. He and DNI Jay Clayton said the more likely cause is the scramble to patch systems before attackers using AI could exploit the flaw. Separately, the FAA paused installation of a router type it believes contributed to last Friday's nationwide ground stop.

**Why it matters.** Java runs a large share of enterprise and government back-end systems. The bigger point is about speed. AI tools now find serious bugs faster than organizations can patch them, and patching on short notice (a fix only takes effect when each JVM restarts) causes its own outages. The cost of discovery has fallen sharply while the cost of fixing has not. For anyone running infrastructure, patch speed and the ability to restart safely are becoming core resilience measures, not routine IT work.

**Confidence.** The CVE and CISA alert are primary sources. The claim that the outages came from patching rather than an attack is the US government's assessment and has not been independently verified.

**Watch.** The release dates of the OpenJDK fix and Oracle's November Critical Patch Update (listed as "TBD" as of 17 Nov). Any confirmed exploitation in the wild. Congressional hearings: incoming Senate Homeland Security chair Maggie Hassan is preparing AI-security hearings.

## 4. The global rate cycle is still tightening

**What happened.** In November, the Reserve Bank of Australia raised its cash rate 25bp to 4.85% (vote 6–3, its fifth hike of this cycle, and it dropped its warning about further increases). The Bank of England raised 25bp to 4% (vote 8–1) and the Czech National Bank 25bp to 4%. Moldova raised 200bp. Mexico unexpectedly cut 25bp to 6.25%, and Brazil paused its cuts with the Selic at 13.75%. In the US, the Fed's October minutes showed most officials expected another hike in 2026 because inflation "had not made much progress" toward 2%. The minutes of the late-October meeting were due 18 Nov. Spot gold fell to about $3,792/oz, its lowest in over a year and more than 25% below its level when the war began in late February.

**Why it matters.** The energy shock from the war, together with tariffs, has kept inflation high in advanced economies, so their central banks are tightening at the same time. That raises borrowing costs for governments with large debts and for emerging markets that borrow in dollars. Gold falling during a war is unusual, and the likely reason is real interest rates: when holding cash or bonds pays a positive return after inflation, the cost of holding gold, which pays nothing, goes up. Mexico's cut shows that economies further from the energy shock can move the other way.

**Confidence.** The RBA decision is confirmed. The BoE and CNB figures come from secondary aggregators and should be checked against the banks' own statements. The gold price is market data.

**Watch.** Contents of the Fed minutes. Next US CPI release. The ECB's December meeting. Fed Governor Lisa Cook's dismissal hearing on 14 Dec. She says she won't attend unless NEC Director Kevin Hassett is removed from the panel. The outcome bears directly on Fed independence.

## 5. Trade blocs are reorganizing around US tariffs

**What happened.** On the APEC sidelines, Canada's Mark Carney and Xi agreed to expand trade through reciprocal tariff relief and new commercial deals, despite US tariff threats against Canada. Carney also expects to meet Trump and says a US–Canada deal is possible. US–Canada negotiations over Section 232 tariffs (50% on steel, 25% on autos) have no deadline. In Manila, Trump announced a bilateral trade agreement and expanded defense cooperation with the Philippines. The Quad (US, Australia, India, Japan) held its first leaders' meeting in over two years. The US also exempted coffee, tea, fruit, beef and fertilizer from IEEPA tariffs (executive order, 14 Nov). The Supreme Court heard arguments on the legality of IEEPA tariffs on 5 Nov.

**Why it matters.** The pattern is two-sided. US allies are making security agreements with Washington and, at the same time, cutting their exposure to US tariffs by trading more with each other and with China. Canada, a G7 member, cutting tariffs with China is the clearest example. The food exemptions show tariffs being rolled back where they raise visible consumer prices. If the Supreme Court rules against IEEPA tariffs, the legal basis for a large part of the current tariff structure would go away.

**Confidence.** The Canada–China and US–Philippines announcements are confirmed. The detailed terms have not been published.

**Watch.** The Supreme Court's IEEPA ruling (date not set). The end of the two-month US–China truce extension, around early January. US–EU framework talks. Switzerland's talks to reduce its 39% tariff.

## Concept: the risk premium that outlasts the risk

When a shipping route, supplier, or country fails once, markets don't simply return to the old price once things work again. They add a **risk premium**: extra cost charged for the possibility that the failure happens again. It appears in insurance rates, inventory levels (companies hold more stock "just in case" rather than "just in time"), the yields lenders demand, and investment decisions (building a pipeline that bypasses the chokepoint even if the chokepoint is open).

That is what Birol is describing for Hormuz. Gulf exports can reach 80% or 100% of pre-war levels while the system costs more to run, because every refiner, utility and finance ministry now plans for a second closure. The same reasoning explains why companies are moving supply chains away from China even when tariffs are paused, and why a central bank that has lost credibility on inflation has to keep rates higher for longer than the data alone would suggest.

**Question to ask of any "things are back to normal" headline:** has the price of insuring against a repeat come back down? If not, the event is still costing money.
